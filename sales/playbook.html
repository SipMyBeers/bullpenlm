THE BULLPEN PLAYBOOK

How to make sales calls when you've never made one before

Read this once. Pin the cheat card to your monitor. Start dialing.

1. The math (so your brain doesn't lie to you)

Cold calling feels brutal because the rejection ratio is huge. Here's

what's normal for a beginner working a clean, pre-loaded list:

StepRealistic ratio
Dials100
Connects (somebody answers — gatekeeper OR buyer)30–35
Conversations with the actual buyer4–8
Booked meetings1–3
Signed pilots0–1 (often after several meetings across weeks)

At $3,750 commission per signed $15K pilot, one close per 100 dials =

$37.50 per dial on net average. That's the number to put on your monitor.

Why this matters: if you make 10 dials and zero buyers picked up, you

didn't fail. You're 10% of the way through the normal ratio. Keep going.

Pace target for week 1: 30 dials/day, 4 days. That's 120 dials.

Expected: 1 meeting booked. By week 4 you should be at 50 dials/day with

1-2 meetings/week and a clearer sense of which industries answer best.

2. What you're selling (in 3 versions)

You'll need the same product explained at 3 different levels of depth,

because gatekeepers, buyers, and C-level execs each want a different

sentence.

A. The gatekeeper version (no jargon)

"It's a tool that helps their team verify their COBOL modernization

work for audits."

Use this when an EA, receptionist, or general operator asks what it's

regarding. Vague enough not to trigger the spam filter, specific enough

to sound like you know who you're calling for.

B. The buyer version (technical, ~30 sec)

"Off-the-shelf AI translation tools silently drop fields in REDEFINES

sections when they convert COBOL. For a claims engine, that's a HIPAA

reporting incident. We do deterministic field-level parity — every

translated field maps 1:1 with cryptographic checksums an auditor can

verify. Runs on their hardware."

Use this with VPs of Application Modernization, Directors of Engineering,

Claims Tech Leads — people who know what REDEFINES means.

C. The exec version (business risk, ~20 sec)

"Their modernization roadmap is auditor-watched. The off-the-shelf AI

translation tools their SI is using have a quiet audit-finding problem

— fields silently get lost. We're the verification layer that catches

it before it shows up in their next regulatory exam."

Use this with CIOs, CTOs, EVP-level. Lead with risk and compliance, not

architecture.

If you can't remember which version to use: default to A on the first

sentence of any call. The other person will signal which depth they want.

3. The call ladder (the only thing that matters)

Every cold call is at most three rungs:


    BUYER REACHED  →  Pitch · Qualify · Book meeting
        ↑
    GATEKEEPER       →  Earn the transfer
        ↑
    PHONE RINGS      →  Dial with intent

Most of your calls will die at the gatekeeper. That's normal. You're

not bad at sales — gatekeepers are paid to filter you out.

Rung 1 — Pick up the phone

Before you dial: stand up, take one deep breath, smile. Your voice

carries energy. A flat, sad voice from your chair = automatic deletion.

Rung 2 — The gatekeeper

Read the gatekeeper drill in BullpenLM (Level 1) before you start. The

short version:

"Hi, this is [your name] calling — could you connect me to [exact name

if you have it], the [exact title]? Thanks."

Three rules:

  • Confidence first. No "I was wondering if maybe…" Just ask.
  • Use the exact name + title. "Jane Smith, VP of App Mod" beats "the

tech person."

  • If they ask what it's regarding: "It's about their COBOL

modernization roadmap — specifically the audit-verification layer.

She's likely expecting a follow-up." (You're not lying; she should be.

It's a real problem.)

If they say "send materials" — most of the time, that's the polite no.

Try once: "Happy to send a one-pager, but a 30-second voice connection

saves both of us an email thread. Is she at her desk?"

If they say no twice, thank them, hang up, dial the next one. Never

argue with a gatekeeper. You'll come back to that account next week.

Rung 3 — The buyer

Buyer picks up. You have 30 seconds before they decide whether to keep

listening.

You: "Hi [first name] — Dylan from Beers Labs. I'll be quick. Your

team is probably evaluating COBOL modernization tools right now with

the major SIs. There's a quiet field-drop problem in those tools that

shows up as audit findings 6 months later. We solve it. Worth 15

minutes Thursday to show your team how it works?"

That's your whole opening. Memorize it. Then shut up and listen.

You did your job by being specific and short. If they say "tell me

more," go to the buyer version (§ 2.B). If they say "Thursday at 2,"

say "perfect, what's the best email for the calendar invite?" and book

it. If they say "not interested" or "we're set," see § 6 (objections).

What "booking a meeting" looks like in practice

You don't need to close the deal on the cold call. The cold call is

won when you have a calendar invite for a 15-minute technical briefing.

That's the only thing you're trying to do. Everything past that is

Beers's job.

4. The 5 plays (you'll use these every day)

Play 1 — The Specific Open

You found a fact in their 10-K, earnings call, LinkedIn post about

their tech transformation slipping. You drop it in the first 15 seconds:

"[First name], your Q3 earnings call mentioned the policy-admin

modernization slipping into 2027. I won't waste your time — calling

because we solve the verification problem that's likely causing the

slip. Got 30 seconds?"

This works because specificity = research = respect. Generic opener

= delete.

Play 2 — The Incumbent Name

If you know they're working with Accenture, IBM, Deloitte, or AWS

Mainframe Modernization (which the BullpenLM dossier will tell you),

name them in the first 20 seconds:

"You're already paying Accenture half a million a year on this. We're

not competing with them — we're the verification layer they don't

have."

Play 3 — The Time Box

Buyers respect reps who respect their time. Never say "do you have a few

minutes?" Say:

"I need 60 seconds. Can I have it?"

Asking for a specific length makes you sound disciplined. "A few minutes"

sounds like an hour.

Play 4 — The Specific Ask

End every call with a specific calendar request:

"Thursday at 2 or Friday at 10?"

NOT "would you be open to a call sometime?" Specific times = closure.

Vague times = nothing happens.

Play 5 — The Quiet

After you ask for the meeting, stop talking. Let the silence sit.

New reps fill silence with apologies, hedges, and downward bids

("…or whenever works for you, no pressure"). That kills the deal.

Ask the question. Count to three in your head. The buyer will fill the

silence. That's the goal.

5. Daily rhythm (the only schedule you need)

9:00–11:30Power block 1: 25 dials. No checking phone, no Slack.
11:30–12:00Recap the morning — log what worked, what didn't.
12:00–1:00Lunch + walk. Don't dial.
1:00–3:30Power block 2: 25 dials. Different industry vertical from morning.
3:30–4:00Run Level 3 (60-sec pitch) in BullpenLM against the toughest call you had today. Rehearse what you'd do differently.
4:00–5:00Calendar invites, follow-up emails, CRM updates.

Why this works: mornings (Eastern time) catch the most buyers at

their desks. Mid-afternoon catches the West Coast. Power blocks of

20-30 dials force you past the "ugh, I don't want to dial" wall. The

BullpenLM drill at end-of-day locks in the lesson while it's fresh.

6. Objection cheat sheet

Most cold-call objections are 5 patterns. Memorize the response, modify

in the moment.

ObjectionResponse
"We already have a solution for that.""Most carriers we talk to are already on Accenture or IBM. We're not a replacement — we're the verification layer underneath. Worth 15 minutes?"
"Send me some materials.""Happy to. The one-pager only makes sense if I know what your modernization stack is. 60 seconds — which SI are you working with?"
"We don't have budget.""Totally understood. The pilot is $15K which usually comes out of an existing modernization line item, not a new one. Worth a quick call to see if your CFO would treat it as insurance, not a new vendor?"
"Why are you calling me?""Because [specific fact about their company — slipping modernization timeline, recent press release, 10-K disclosure] suggests we solve a problem your team has right now. I won't waste your time — 30 seconds and you decide."
"I'm not the right person.""Got it — who at [Company] owns the COBOL modernization verification work? I'll save us both a step." (90% of the time they'll give you a name or transfer you.)

Rule: Never argue with an objection. Acknowledge → reframe → ask for

a small next step. That's it.

7. The handoff signals (when to throw it to Dylan)

You're a cold-caller, not a closer. Your job ends at "calendar

invite booked." Hand the deal to Dylan the moment you hit any of these

signals:

  • Buyer says yes to a 15-minute briefing → invite Dylan to the

calendar event, brief him beforehand, you can sit in if you want.

  • Buyer asks technical questions you can't answer ("how does your

field-mapping engine handle COBOL REDEFINES with nested OCCURS?") →

"Great question — I want to make sure you get a precise answer. Our

CTO can walk you through it Thursday."

  • Buyer asks about pricing past the $15K pilot → "$15K is the pilot.

Expansion pricing depends on scope — I'll have our CEO walk you

through it on the briefing."

  • Buyer asks for a signed contract, redlines, or legal terms →

immediate Dylan handoff.

Don't try to be the technical expert. That's not what you were hired

for. Your value is volume + persistence + booking meetings. Beers

handles the close.

8. How to log a call so you get paid

§ 3 of the agreement requires you to prove first contact. BullpenLM

makes this automatic — but you have to actually use it:

  • Set your REP name in the top-right of the floor before your first

call of the day. (One time, persists in localStorage.)

  • Click the prospect card in the floor before you dial. The card

opens with the prospect's full dossier (research, contacts, openers).

  • Hit 🔴 Record on the dossier the moment the buyer picks up. The

recording proves first contact via timestamp + your voice + the

prospect's voice.

  • After the call, the auto-debrief writes the transcript, extracts

any new contact info the buyer mentioned, and updates the call log

under your REP name.

  • Update pipeline status in the dossier (Sent → VM → Gatekeeper →

Qualified → Briefing Booked → Signed). This is what unlocks Beers's

awareness that you closed something.

No log = no commission. The CRM record is the contract for who

originated the account. If you skip steps 2–4, your claim under § 3 of

the agreement is weak.

10. The non-negotiables

A short list of things that will get you fired (or just embarrassed):

  • Don't oversell. Never promise a feature that doesn't exist. If

the buyer asks "does it do X?" and you don't know, say "let me check

with our CTO and follow up in the calendar invite." That answer

builds trust. Making up a yes destroys it.

  • Don't disclose pricing past the $15K pilot. Expansion and

enterprise pricing is Beers's conversation, not yours.

  • Don't trash competitors. Saying "Accenture sucks" makes you sound

small. Saying "Accenture is great at integration, we just handle the

verification layer they don't" makes you sound senior.

  • Don't cold-call House Accounts (see house-accounts.md). Zero

commission on those even if you close them, and Beers will be pissed

you stepped on his work.

  • Don't cold-call Partner-Motion accounts (the SIs and tool

companies). Different sales conversation entirely. If you call

Accenture and pitch them a pilot, you'll damage a partnership Beers

has been building.

  • Don't ghost a booked meeting. If you have a calendar event, you

show up — even if you're not running it. Buyers remember reps who

disappear.

  • Don't say "I'm just the SDR." You're a salesperson. Own the call.

11. The mindset

A cold call is a coin flip dressed up as a job. The buyer's mood

yesterday determines half your outcome. You only control what you can

control:

  • The number of dials you make.
  • The specificity of your opener.
  • Whether you ask for the meeting at the end.
  • Whether you log the call.

Everything else is variance. The reps who win aren't the most charming

— they're the ones who dial when nobody's making them dial.

One last thing: every "no" gets you closer to a "yes." The math is

on your side as long as you keep dialing.
